At its centre is the question of how knowledge, identity and communicative norms are produced and changed in linguistic practice, and how these processes can be described under digital conditions. Digitality plays a double role here. It changes the objects of linguistics, from web forums and platform communication to interaction with language-generating systems. And it changes the procedures: digital tools structure data, set units of analysis and help determine what counts as a finding. My research programme therefore combines the study of linguistic practice with a reflexive methodology of its digital capture.

My concept of language is cultural-analytic and usage-based: I understand language as a practice in which knowledge and meaning are produced situationally and solidify into patterns of use. Two guiding questions follow. What can contemporary German tell us about how a digitally shaped society orders knowledge, marks belonging and enforces or shifts communicative norms? And how should language be described when platforms, tools and generative systems increasingly take part in producing it alongside humans?

## Corpus pragmatics and data philology {#corpus-pragmatics}

I call my methodological position *data philology*: computing and reading intertwine, and every tool is examined for the research logic it brings into the analysis. I developed this position in my dissertation, when it became clear that corpus tools and their default settings already decide what counts as a finding. This led to my own Python implementations of classic procedures, an open, modular toolbox for corpus-pragmatic analysis and visualisation, and corpus infrastructures I built myself.

The next frontier is multimodality. As soon as image and layout are part of language use, text-based procedures reach their limits. Within the International Max Planck Research School for Multimodal Digital Humanities, I am building a multimodal corpus of the youth magazine *Bravo* and testing procedures that treat corpora as text-image assemblages. I treat large language models the way I previously treated word embeddings: as procedures that have to be tested. To this end I am developing an evaluation protocol that documents model decisions and measures them against manual annotation. A methodology differs from a toolbox in that it includes the decision against the advanced procedure.

## Discourse, knowledge, society {#discourse}

In my dissertation I examined how HIV/AIDS is conceptualised linguistically in German-speaking Switzerland since the infection became treatable and seemingly disappeared from public attention. The study rests on two corpora I built myself, from press texts and an online forum, totalling around 600 million tokens. From the findings I developed the concept of *discourse latency*: an answer to the question of what happens to discourses when public interest fades, and how stereotypes and heterodox knowledge are reactivated in new arenas. Studies on Wikipedia, conspiracy theories, 1980s protest communication and the linguistic construction of sexuality build on this.

## Postdigital writing cultures {#writing-cultures}

My habilitation project examines how generative AI reconfigures writing practices, products and ideologies in postdigital environments. I read these changes not as a rupture but as an intensification of tendencies already present in digital writing: the distribution of text production across human and non-human participants, the negotiation of authorship and authenticity, the entanglement of automation and regimes of evaluation. The theoretical frame is the concept of assemblage: writing as a configuration of people, technologies, infrastructures and regimes of evaluation in which agency is distributed.

Along the triad of ideologies, practices and products, I reconstruct language- and media-ideological positionings from guidelines, public debates and interviews, study situated writing processes multimodally through screen, video and process data, and analyse the resulting texts with corpus and text linguistics. The material comes from school, academic and institutional writing in the German-speaking educational space. I understand citizen science as a linguistic method, not merely a format for outreach.
